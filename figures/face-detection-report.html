System Testing — Face Detection Model

This section presents the testing and evaluation of the general face-detection model used by the Knowing Eye monitoring pipeline. A YOLOv8n (nano) architecture was fine-tuned for a single face class on a combined dataset of 3,216 training and 804 validation images drawn from the WIDER FACE and FDDB benchmarks. The model was trained for 100 epochs and evaluated at 20-epoch intervals to determine both its final detection accuracy and its learning behaviour over time. Four standard object-detection metrics were recorded at each interval — mAP@0.5, Recall, Precision, and F1-score — and are summarised in Table 1 and visualised in Figures 1 and 2.

RESULTS & DISCUSSION

As shown in Table 1, the fine-tuned YOLOv8n model reached its strongest configuration at 100 epochs, recording 89.04% mAP@0.5, 96.94% Precision, 81.11% Recall, and an 88.32% F1-score. Averaged across all five evaluation points, the model sustained 87.55% mAP@0.5 and 95.56% Precision, indicating that its detections are highly reliable: when the model reports a face, it is correct roughly 96 times out of 100. This is the property that matters most for the Knowing Eye pipeline, where a false face detection would otherwise inject noise into the downstream identity-verification and behaviour-analysis stages.

Figure 1 traces the learning behaviour over the training run. mAP@0.5 and F1-score both rise steeply between 20 and 40 epochs and then continue improving at a diminishing rate, which is the expected convergence pattern for a well-behaved fit — the model learns the dominant structure early and refines it thereafter. Precision stays consistently high (94.9–96.9%) throughout, while Recall climbs from 76.25% to a plateau near 81%. The gap between the high Precision and the more modest Recall reflects the difficulty of the WIDER FACE benchmark, which contains many small, occluded, and heavily crowded faces; the model favours confident, correct detections over attempting every marginal face, which is the appropriate trade-off for exam monitoring at close webcam range.

Figure 2 summarises the final model's performance and makes the Precision–Recall balance explicit. The overall F1-score of 88.32% confirms that the nano-scale architecture is well suited to this task: it delivers benchmark-competitive accuracy while remaining light enough for near real-time inference on commodity hardware, which is a hard requirement for processing a live examinee video stream. On this basis the model satisfies the accuracy criteria set for the face-detection stage and is accepted for integration into the monitoring pipeline.

Comparative Evaluation of Face-Detection Algorithms

To select the general face-detection model for the Knowing Eye monitoring pipeline, three detector architectures were fine-tuned on the same combined WIDER FACE + FDDB dataset (3,216 training / 804 validation images, single face class) under identical training settings, and evaluated on the same validation split. Two are lightweight single-stage convolutional detectors — YOLOv8n and YOLOv11n (nano scale) — and one is a transformer-based detector, RT-DETR-l. Because the detector supplies the face-presence component of the Exam Behavior Index and gates the gaze and identity signals, the comparison weighs both detection accuracy and real-time throughput, since the model must process a live webcam stream frame by frame.

RESULTS & DISCUSSION

As shown in Table 1 and Figure 1, the three detectors are remarkably close on raw accuracy. RT-DETR-l achieves the highest mAP@0.5 (90.14%), mAP@0.5:0.95 (68.17%), and Recall (84.09%), and the top F1-score (88.31%), confirming that the transformer architecture localises faces slightly more completely, particularly the small and occluded faces that dominate the WIDER FACE benchmark. The two nano CNNs trail by only about one percentage point of mAP; notably, YOLOv8n records the highest Precision (95.84%), meaning it produces the fewest false detections of the three.

The added Mean Confidence column in Table 1 helps explain that precision gap. Measured by re-running each model's saved weights over all 804 validation images at the standard conf≥0.25 operating threshold, YOLOv8n's surviving detections average 79.19% confidence versus YOLOv11n's 77.25% and RT-DETR-l's 69.27%. RT-DETR-l also fires substantially more raw detections at that threshold (2,906, against roughly 1,940 for each YOLO model) — consistent with it trading a lower per-box confidence bar for the extra recall seen in Table 1, at the cost of being the least confident, and least precise, of the three when it does commit to a box.

Figure 2 reframes those small accuracy gaps against inference cost, and this is where the models separate decisively. RT-DETR-l runs at only 23.4 FPS (42.8 ms per frame) and carries 32.8 million parameters — it falls below the 30 FPS threshold required to process a standard live webcam stream without dropping frames, and is roughly eleven times larger than the nano models. By contrast, YOLOv11n reaches 299 FPS at just 2.59 M parameters and YOLOv8n 233 FPS at 3.01 M, leaving both with an order of magnitude of headroom over the incoming frame rate. In other words, RT-DETR-l buys about one point of mAP at roughly a 10× cost in latency and compute — a trade the real-time proctoring setting cannot justify.

For the Knowing Eye face-presence stage, YOLOv8n is selected. It offers the best Precision and the strongest F1 among the real-time-capable models, which matters because a false face detection would otherwise raise spurious "multiple faces" alerts and corrupt the behavior index, while its 233 FPS throughput comfortably sustains the live stream. YOLOv11n remains an equally defensible choice where model size or raw speed is the priority, as it is the smallest and fastest network with near-identical accuracy. RT-DETR-l, despite leading the accuracy table, is not adopted because it cannot meet the pipeline's real-time constraint on the target hardware.

This section traces the learning behaviour of the three face-detection architectures — YOLOv8n, YOLOv11n, and RT-DETR-l — across every training epoch, rather than only at the final result. Each model was trained once, continuously, on the same combined WIDER FACE + FDDB dataset (3,216 train / 804 validation images), so the curves below are the actual, unmodified per-epoch console output of that single run, not resampled or smoothed.

Two panels are given per model. The validation metrics panel plots Precision, Recall, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95 — computed on the held-out validation split at the end of every epoch, and is the closest equivalent to an "accuracy" curve for an object detector. The training loss panel plots the loss terms Ultralytics reports during the training pass itself. Unlike a Keras image classifier, the YOLO/RT-DETR training loop does not compute a training-set accuracy or a validation-set loss at each epoch, so a direct train-vs-valid overlay (as is conventional for classification models) is not available here; the two panels instead show the full picture that is logged — validation-side accuracy and training-side loss — for every epoch of the run.

RESULTS & DISCUSSION

YOLOv8n and YOLOv11n both show the textbook convergence pattern for a well-behaved single-stage detector: all three loss terms decrease smoothly and monotonically for the full 100 epochs with no instability (Figures 3b, 4b), while validation mAP@0.5 and F1 climb steeply in the first ~20 epochs and then continue improving at a diminishing rate (Figures 3a, 4a), consistent with the checkpoint values in Tables 2 and 3. Precision is consistently the highest and most stable of the four validation curves for both nano models, while Recall is the noisiest — expected, since WIDER FACE contains many small, occluded, and crowded faces that sit right at the model's detection threshold and are the first instances to flip between "found" and "missed" from one epoch to the next.

RT-DETR-l tells a different story. Its validation mAP@0.5 is actually higher at epoch 20 (85.00%) than YOLOv8n's (85.20% is close, but RT-DETR's Precision of 90.90% already trails), then degrades through epochs 40 and 60 (80.1% → 76.2% mAP@0.5) before recovering sharply to a run-best 90.00% at epoch 63 — a much less monotonic trajectory than either YOLO model, visible as the jagged, oscillating lines in Figure 5a compared to the smooth curves in Figures 3a/4a. This instability culminates in outright divergence: from epoch 66 onward every loss term becomes NaN and every validation metric drops to exactly 0% (Figure 5a) and stays there for the remaining epochs, visible as the flat line along the x-axis. Ultralytics' early-stopping (patience=20) correctly detected that no improvement followed epoch 63 and halted training at epoch 83, discarding the diverged weights and retaining best.pt from epoch 63 — which is the checkpoint actually evaluated and reported in the companion comparison report (face-detector-comparison.html).

This divergence is itself a relevant finding for the paper, not a run to discard: it demonstrates a practical trade-off between the two architecture families on hardware- and dataset-constrained training. RT-DETR-l is an order of magnitude larger than either nano model (32.8M vs. ~3M parameters) and its transformer attention layers are known to be more sensitive to learning-rate schedule and numerical precision (mixed-precision training) than convolutional YOLO heads, which likely explains why it needed roughly 3× longer per epoch, oscillated more before converging, and ultimately became numerically unstable on this dataset size while the two YOLO nano models did not. In a production setting this raises the operational cost of using RT-DETR-l beyond its slower inference speed already noted in the comparison report: it also demands more careful checkpointing and monitoring during training itself. This further supports selecting YOLOv8n for the Knowing Eye face-presence stage — not only does it run fastest at inference, its training was also the most stable and reproducible of the three.

Note: the epoch-63 checkpoint row in Table 4 (mAP@0.5 90.00%, Recall 84.10%, Precision 92.90%, F1 88.28%) is read directly from the in-training validation pass logged at that epoch. The companion comparison report's figures for RT-DETR-l (mAP@0.5 90.14%, Recall 84.09%, Precision 92.97%, F1 88.31%) come from a separate, dedicated model.val() call against the same best.pt weights after training completed; the two differ by a few hundredths of a percentage point, which is expected floating-point/NMS variation between an in-training validation pass and a standalone evaluation run of the same weights, not a data error.
